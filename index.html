Our read In Jeremy's words
The one idea to keep

Stop starting with the tool.
Start with the gap.

“I learned the tools because I needed to fill the gaps. Not the other way around. Notice the gap, talk to the tool, solve it, and move on with your life. That's the pattern.”— Jeremy
What stayed with us01 — 05
01

You can ship through chat now

Jeremy is a creative director who writes no code and has no plans to start. Every tool he showed today, from a personal dashboard to a prospect-intelligence tool, came from the same place: a conversation, not a codebase.

“It's just another tab on the thing you're already using. Everything I show you is built basically by chat — me saying: here's my problem, here's my gap, let's figure out a way to fill it. If you've been putting off Claude because it feels too technical, that's the wrong reason.”

02

The best ideas start as small annoyances

The best ideas here didn't start as ambitious platforms. They started as personal irritations. Jem Hub, which now stands in for five separate lifestyle apps, began with nothing more than a daily annoyance.

“The movement from my brain asking what time it is in New York, the mouse-movement up to that app — that's a gap. So I built a tool that fills all the little question-mark gaps. It's my number-one tab now, before my email every day.”

03

Context beats skill files

Hand-built skill files — the saved instructions you build once and expect Claude to reuse — are already getting replaced by something looser: just feeding the model who you are and trusting it to hold onto that.

“The model's getting better and better, so skills are going to go away. The thing you actually need is your context. I have a voice file — how I write, how I deliver — built from 50 transcripts of me. Anything I make comes out in my voice.”

04

Don't organise it. Search it.

Folders are for our mushy human eyes. Stop building version-control systems and file structures; treat the model like your brain, and retrieve. It organises and backs up better than you will.

“I don't organise it — Claude is much better at that than me. There's no folder structure in your brain. I just go to the search and type what I'm looking for, the same way Finder works. Coming up with how to fill the gap is your job. Filing isn't.”

05

The models have converged

Model capability has stopped being the thing worth arguing about. What's left is closer to a lifestyle choice than a technical one.

“It's not a matter of who's going to win — they'll all have the same tool. It comes down to cost, and your appetite for that company's ethics or politics. I'm leaning heavily to Claude. I like Anthropic as a company, and I enjoy using their tools from a non-technical perspective.”

Field notes

“My biggest hack is not typing — it's talking to it, through the app or the mic. It synthesises your thoughts.”  ·  “I never use the latest model for most things — last month's model is more than good enough for anything you'd ever want to build.”

06

Jeremy's 10 · 80 · 10 rule

Prototype and production are not the same thing — and Jeremy has a simple rule for exactly where the line sits.

10%
Human · Context & the gap
80%
The machine · Faster, cheaper, skills you don't have
10%
Human · QA, security, money, scale

“Humans are bookended. The first 10% is us giving context and describing the gap. The 80% gets us there faster and cheaper. The last 10% is me going: is this what I asked for? Get a dev in for three things — security, any money moving in any direction, and scale.”

What Jeremy actually built

One throughline: every one started from a specific, personal pain point.
Jem Hub: a personal dashboard swallowing several lifestyle apps into one live page
Six Degrees of Tennis: a data hobby project connecting any two players
Prospect-intelligence report: built from a raw LinkedIn export in ~20 minutes
Kids' birthday invites: a live business, personalised video, Stripe payments
Client training hub: auto-built each week from the session's Granola transcript
The pattern: concrete problem in, working prototype out

“Learning to see a gap and fill it is the biggest skill any of us could learn right now — bigger than any MBA or degree. You don't need to learn the tools. You need to get good at seeing the gaps. They're everywhere.”

— Jeremy
The bottom line

AI earns its keep when it's aimed at a sharply defined workflow gap. For creative and non-technical people, that's a new mode of working: prototype tools, automate delivery, test product ideas yourself, then bring in specialists only when risk, scale, or money demand it.
